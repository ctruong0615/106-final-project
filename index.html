165 Years of Decline

From 1850 to 2014, surface ocean pH fell steadily — an acceleration unprecedented in 300 million years of Earth history. A drop of just 0.1 pH units represents a 26% increase in ocean acidity, due to the logarithmic nature of the pH scale.

The orange line marks the actual 1850 pre-industrial baseline computed from the data — a level the ocean will not return to in any human lifetime.

02

But Not Everywhere Equally

In 1850, every ocean region sat at nearly the same pH. By 2014, they had dramatically diverged. The Arctic — where cold water physically absorbs CO₂ more efficiently — shows the steepest decline.

This is the surprise: the regions acidifying fastest are also the ones that support the most fragile, cold-adapted ecosystems — with the least biological buffer against change.

Arctic (>60°N)

The Fastest-Changing Ocean

Cold polar water physically dissolves CO₂ more efficiently than warm water, making the Arctic the single most acidified region on the planet. Its pH has dropped by more than 0.11 units since 1850 — the steepest decline of any latitude band.

As sea ice retreats and exposes fresh surface area, the feedback accelerates. Pteropods and cold-adapted shell-builders are already living near their physiological limits.

N. Temperate (30–60°N)

The World's Fisheries Under Pressure

The North Temperate band spans the Atlantic and Pacific fishing grounds that feed billions of people. A steady, relentless drop of roughly 0.09 pH units is playing out across the same waters where oysters, scallops, and herring reproduce.

Upwelling zones along western coasts — already corrosive — are intensifying, bringing deep acidic water closer to the surface than at any point in recorded history.

Tropical (30°S–30°N)

Coral Reefs on a Knife's Edge

Warmer tropical water holds less dissolved CO₂, so the absolute pH drop here is smaller than at the poles. But the baseline pH is also higher — and coral reefs need that buffer. Aragonite saturation is falling toward the threshold below which reef-building becomes impossible.

Combined with heat-driven bleaching, even modest acidification creates a dual stressor that many reef systems cannot survive.

S. Temperate (60–30°S)

Open Water, Wide Impact

The Southern Temperate band is dominated by open ocean with few land masses to interrupt circulation. The Antarctic Circumpolar Current sweeps acidified water eastward around the globe, distributing the signal across both hemispheres.

The Patagonian shelf — one of the most productive fisheries on Earth — sits squarely in this band and is already seeing pH readings that threaten juvenile squid and toothfish populations.

Southern Ocean (<60°S)

A Sink That Cannot Stop Absorbing

The Southern Ocean absorbs roughly 40% of all oceanic CO₂ uptake — far more than its surface area would suggest. That efficiency comes at a cost: it is acidifying faster than virtually any other region outside the Arctic.

Antarctic krill, which sustain the entire food web from penguins to whales, are losing their calcified egg capsules at lower pH. A collapse here would reverberate through the entire Southern Hemisphere ecosystem.

05

What Acidification Means for Life

🦪

Shellfish & Corals

Below pH 7.8, aragonite — the mineral that builds shells and coral skeletons — begins to dissolve. Pacific oyster hatcheries have already suffered mass die-offs attributed to acidification.

🦐

Pteropods

These "sea butterflies" form the base of polar food webs. Their shells visibly dissolve when exposed to projected near-future pH levels in laboratory experiments — putting entire Arctic ecosystems at risk.

🐟

Fish Behavior

Elevated CO₂ disrupts fish sensory systems and decision-making. Studies show reef fish raised in acidified water swim toward predators rather than away — a behavioral reversal with deadly consequences.

06

The Ocean Cannot Buffer Forever

The data tells a clear story: the ocean has been absorbing humanity's chemical consequences for 165 years. The pH we measured — globally, regionally, and down to the 5° grid cell — has already crossed thresholds that took hundreds of millions of years to evolve around.

Project Writeup

1. What have you done so far?

Firstly, we obtained ocean pH from the IPSL-CM6A-LR climate model and aggregated the monthly timescales into annual averages. Additionally, we binned regions into 5-degree latitude/longitude sections and took only the surface pH data. After obtaining this information, we created a D3.js line graph depicting the trend of decreasing ocean pH between 1980 and 2014. Also, we made an interactive toggle that computes and colors the acidification deficit instantly. Finally, we made a static line chart and a static global map to show how different regions have changed since 1850.

2. What will be the most challenging part of your project to design and why?

The most difficult part of the assignment will be developing multiple, simultaneously interacting views that correlate the pH values with the actual population numbers of certain marine species. Creating such views entails maintaining the complex state in D3.js where changing any one view instantaneously changes another related visualization without any lagging or freezing. In addition, the biological measurements and their connection with physical pH readings can lead to visual noise in the plot, and we should be careful about the implementation of this functionality. Maintaining a balance between technical constraints and expressiveness criteria is going to be extremely challenging for us.
