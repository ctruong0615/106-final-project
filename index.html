01

165 Years of Decline

From 1850 to 2014, the pH levels of the surface ocean have been falling steadily, which is quite unexpected when comparinng the history of 300 million years on Earth. While each drop of a measly 0.1 pH units seems insignificant at first glance, this actually represents a 26% increase in ocean acidity, which is only that significant due to pH being logarithmic rather than linear. As a result, small numbers on this graph indicate that there are massive chemical changes.

Here, the orange line marks the actual 1850 pre-industrial baseline that is computed from our data. This represents the stable ocean that had existed for millions of years before industrialization was widespread. With the amount of burning and pollution that is produced, the ocean will never be able to revert back to its original pH level.

02

But Not Everywhere Equally

In 1850, every ocean region sat at nearly the same pH. However, by 2014, they had dramatically diverged. Particularly, places where cold water is abundant, like the Arctic or the Bering Sea, physically absorbs CO₂ more efficiently, which shows the steepest decline in pH levels. Similarly, we can understand this concept by observing a cold soda can, which is able to preserve carbonation longer than warm soda cans because it can hold gas in more efficiently.

Unfortunately, the coldest regions in the ocean are often the places with very fragile ecosystems, so creatures such as the Arctic cod suffer from change greatly because they never needed to evolve to fight against the acidifying ocean.

04

What Acidification Means for Life

🦪

Shellfish & Walrus Diets

Below a pH level of 7.8, the materials that shellfish need for their shells (aragonite), dissolve. With walruses consuming bivalves and clams as a primary source of their diet in the Bering Sea, this is concerning as walruses begin to have a lack of food to eat. As a result, the walrus populations will have to migrate elsewhere, which disrupts the traditional Inuit subsistence hunting of them, making it harder to find food and materials.

🦐

Pteropods & Salmon

Polar bears often eat fish like salmon and Pacific cod to survive; however, ocean acidification contributes to pteropods' shells dissolving. As a result, fish populations may die out due to a lack of pteropods, which will threaten food security and resources of the Inuit people that come from hunting fish and polar bears.

🐟

Fish Behavior

Elevated CO₂ disrupts fish sensory systems and decision-making. Research shows that fish that grow up in acidified waters often swim toward predators instead of swimming away, which is an issue for Inuit people because it makes hunting fish traditionally too unreliable and unpredictable (seeing how common migration patterns of marine life are different).

05

One Sea.
One People's Lifeline.

The Bering Sea supplies an overwhelming 45% of all US seafood by volume, which is approximately $1.8 billion annually. This may not initially seem too apparent to many people living in the United States, but the impact that indigenous people experience is much greater. It is life-threatening for the Inuit people, who have survived solely on marine life for thousands of years; they lack access to supermarkets, so preventing pH levels from decreasing and acidifying the ocean are especially important to ensure they don't die.

Unfortunately, we see that the Bering Sea has lost over 0.15 pH units since pre-industrial times, which is significantly faster than the global ocean average. Since 1984, half of that devastating drop can be observed, which shows that since 1850, the pH levels have been dropping more and more rapidly.

Sources: NOAA/AFSC Environmental & Fisheries Data; Bristol Bay Economic Development Corp.; ADFG Stock Assessment; pH computed from IPSL-CM6A-LR climate model.

Project Writeup

1. What have you done so far?

Firstly, we obtained ocean pH from the IPSL-CM6A-LR climate model and aggregated the monthly timescales into annual averages. Additionally, we binned regions into 5-degree latitude/longitude sections and took only the surface pH data. After obtaining this information, we created a D3.js line graph depicting the trend of decreasing ocean pH between 1980 and 2014. Also, we made an interactive toggle that computes and colors the acidification deficit instantly. Finally, we made a static line chart and a static global map to show how different regions have changed since 1850.

2. What will be the most challenging part of your project to design and why?

The most difficult part of the assignment will be developing multiple, simultaneously interacting views that correlate the pH values with the actual population numbers of certain marine species. Creating such views entails maintaining the complex state in D3.js where changing any one view instantaneously changes another related visualization without any lagging or freezing. In addition, the biological measurements and their connection with physical pH readings can lead to visual noise in the plot, and we should be careful about the implementation of this functionality. Maintaining a balance between technical constraints and expressiveness criteria is going to be extremely challenging for us.
